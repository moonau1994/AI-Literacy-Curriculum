Human Capapbilities & Limitations with AI Use

Intro

As you will see throughout the rest of this module, when you use AI tools, you are allowing it a level of control and agency over your learning experience and the content you create. It is important to understand when and how to use these tools to avoid academic misconduct, and work that does not reflect your own skills and understanding. AI does more than put words on a screen. It changes the way you interact with information, information resources, and your own brain.

Bateson Introduction

Anthropologist Gregory Bateson's cybernetic-inspired theory of mind posits that minds are made of interacting parts, parts that can be living, non-living, human, and non-human. By his definition, a human and AI tool working together count as one collaborative mind system. Here are three of Bateson's criteria that do well in explaining his theory of mind:

Closing / Transition to MIT Paper

When using AI, you're doing more than just using a tool. It is a system of interacting parts, feedback loops, and translated signals that you are now a part of. To put it plainly, you're granting a digital mind control over how you understand information, gather information, and agency over the work you produce. It is important to understand not only the capabilities and limitations of all the tools you use in school, but also the capabilities and limitations it places over you as you incorporate it into your workflow. Some Historical Context

Mathematician, philosopher, and computer scientist Norbert Wiener coined the term Cybernetics in the 1950s. Cybernetics is the study of controlling the flow of information in systems with feedback loops. These systems can be biological, mechanical, living, non-living, or a group of both working together in tandem. Cybernetics lays the foundation for understanding how flows of information impact behavior in self-correcting systems. The philosophical implications of Cybernetic theory place humans and non-humans, living and nonliving, in a symbiotic relationship that is often overlooked. This idea of feedback and self-correction still underlies how many AI systems are trained and refined today.

Computer scientist and cognitive scientist John McCarthy, known as the "founding father" of AI, coined the term Artificial Intelligence in the late 1950s. His work led to groundbreaking research at Dartmouth College that helped establish AI as a serious field of research. McCarthy held the optimistic belief that machines could hold beliefs in a way often only attributed to humans (CITE MCCARTHY ASCRIBING...).

Interestingly enough, McCarthy's choice of the term "Artificial Intelligence" wasn't a neutral one. By the mid-1950s, Wiener's presence in the field of computing was seen as frustrating, due in part to his moral and pessimistic views on the techno-utopian dreams of his colleagues; Wiener warned of the loss of jobs and human agency if democratic institutions did not properly govern this type of technology. McCarthy's move to coining Artificial Intelligence as a new term was partly a way of distancing his research from cybernetics and Wiener personally, to avoid a choice between deferring to Wiener as an authority or constantly clashing with his views on a moral and personal level. From a technical point of view, the two also disagreed on approach: McCarthy favored a top-down focus on symbolic reasoning and problem-solving, while Wiener and the cyberneticists championed a bottom-up view centered on feedback, adaptation, and biological inspiration. This heated rivalry is significant, as decades later, modern neural networks and deep learning trace their roots back to the cybernetic tradition McCarthy sought to break away from.
