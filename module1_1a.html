Cybernetics & Artificial Intelligence

There are many different definitions that categorize Artificial Intelligence, and disagreements over what the term should mean is not a modern problem. This problem goes back to the field's founding. Scholars and dictionaries offer workable starting points: Merriam-Webster defines Artificial Intelligence as "the capability of computer systems or algorithms to imitate intelligent human behavior" (MERRIAM WEBSTER CITE). But the people who built this field never fully agreed on what they were naming, or even on what question they were trying to answer. As we'll see, two of AI's founding figures, John McCarthy and Norbert Wiener, looked at the same basic problem of how we understand machines that behave in purposefully intelligent ways, and arrived at fundamentally different starting points. Understanding this disagreement between these two scientists defines AI better than any single definition, because it shows us the ambiguity you'll hear in conversations about AI today. Is AI a tool, a mind, a system, or a process?

Mathematician, philosopher, and computer scientist Norbert Wiener coined the term Cybernetics in the 1950s. Cybernetics is the study of controlling the flow of information in systems with feedback loops. These systems can be biological, mechanical, living, non-living, or a group of both working together in tandem. Cybernetics lays the foundation for understanding how flows of information impact behavior in self-correcting systems. The philosophical implications of Cybernetic theory place humans and non-humans, living and nonliving, in a symbiotic relationship that is often overlooked. This idea of feedback and self-correction still underlies how many AI systems are trained and refined today.

Computer scientist and cognitive scientist John McCarthy, known as the "founding father" of AI, coined the term Artificial Intelligence in the late 1950s. His work led to groundbreaking research at Dartmouth College that helped establish AI as a serious field of research. McCarthy held the optimistic belief that machines could hold beliefs in a way often only attributed to humans (CITE MCCARTHY ASCRIBING...). McCarthy's choice of the term "Artificial Intelligence" wasn't just a neutral one. By the mid-1950s, McCarthy wanted distance from cybernetics and from Wiener specifically. This was a fact stated by McCarthy himself, writing that inventing a new term was partly a way to escape associations with cybernetics, whose emphasis on analog feedback McCarthy believed misguided, and to avoid a position where he would either have to defer to Wiener "as a guru" or else constantly argue with him (MCCARTHY CITATION). From a technical standpoint, the two also differed in approach. McCarthy gravitated toward what we might loosely call a "nuts and bolts" view, asking how capacities we ordinarily describe using mental language can be formally represented and attributed to computational systems. Wiener and the cybernetic tradition, meanwhile, asked a bigger-picture question: how does purposive behavior emerge through feedback, communication, and control across systems as a whole? This distinction is a useful heuristic for understanding differences between these two approaches, but the boundaries between both fields are far more porous than just a top-down versus bottom-up divergence might suggest.

Norbert Wiener was deeply engaged with the ethical and political stakes of automation, having written about labor displacement, the erosion of human agency, and the dangers of building efficient machines whose purposes were not fully understood. Sound familiar? That makes Wiener's role in AI innovation important in the field of "AI ethics." Cybernetics itself carried a developed set of concerns about automation, labor, and responsibility that wouldn't resurface prominently in "AI" discourse for decades.

Decades later, modern neural networks and deep learning would draw heavily on ideas rooted in the cybernetic tradition. Traditions of AI and cybernetics never fully separated; they split, recombined, and resurfaced in different forms as the field evolved.

With that short background in mind, let's dig a bit deeper into the "nuts and bolts" definitions of AI and its subcategories. This will help us build a more well-rounded understanding of day-to-day AI use and the many different words and concepts being thrown around in this fast-moving field of "thinking" machines.
