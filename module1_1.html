Step 1 — Read

There is no simple, reliably calculable way to define intelligence that doesn't oversimplify the brain functions of living organisms. Intelligence quotient (IQ) tests may capture a narrow slice of what characterizes an intelligent person, but they fail to capture the full range of characteristics that define intelligence.

Many of us have experienced that moment in school when a test score became a defining feature of how we understood our own thinking skills. From pop quizzes to final exams, our perceived intelligence is largely shaped by our ability to memorize information. This approach oversimplifies what it means to be intelligent in spaces outside of a learning environment. Intelligence isn't just a test score; it encompasses exposure to knowledge and consistent experience in what we might call a practice of applied knowledge, one that unfolds in novel situations where we connect new information to what we already know.

Human intelligence has been defined in many ways. Merriam-Webster dictionary describes it as "the ability to learn or understand things or to deal with new or difficult situations." Psychologist Linda Gottfredson defines it as "the ability to reason, plan, solve problems, think abstractly, comprehend complex ideas, learn quickly, and learn from experience" (GOTTFREDSON CITE). Gilles Gignac describes it as the "maximal capacity to achieve a novel goal successfully using perceptual-cognitive processes" (GIGNAC CITE).

Across these definitions, a common thread emerges: the ability to solve a new problem by drawing on existing knowledge, improvising in unfamiliar situations using what you already know. Human intelligence, then, is underpinned by one's ability to perceive, interact with, and think about the world, whether through visual, auditory, or mental processes, or some combination of all three. Our capacity to integrate this information and respond to it is what constitutes human intelligence.

As you work through these modules, take note of the perceptual and cognitive strengths you already show, and seek out new experiences that test what you know and reveal what more you have to learn. Work out those metacognitive skills!

Metacognition Defined

Metacognition is essentially thinking about thinking, and making intentional decisions about your learning. These decisions show up in how you approach a task like reading, note-taking, writing, or test-taking.

It also involves choosing the right tools for the task, drawing upon your past knowledge and experience, and engaging in problem-solving.
